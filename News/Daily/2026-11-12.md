## 1. Russia and Ukraine agree in principle to a major prisoner exchange

Russian and Ukrainian negotiators ended two days of US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi with an agreement in principle on what could become the war's largest prisoner exchange, with the number of captives to be determined on Thursday. Both sides reported progress, but no breakthrough emerged over territory, security guarantees or a possible Black Sea shipping ceasefire, and attacks continued during the negotiations.

**Why it matters:** The exchange would be a concrete humanitarian result from the first direct negotiations in nine months, while the remaining gaps show that a comprehensive peace settlement is still distant.

## 2. Democrat Scott Colom wins historic Mississippi Senate upset

Scott Colom defeated Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by about 4,600 votes, becoming Mississippi's first Black US senator since Reconstruction and its first Democratic senator since 1982. The result expanded the Democratic Senate majority to 55–44, with one race still undecided, after a campaign centred on living costs and fuel prices.

**Why it matters:** The result deepens the scale of the Democratic midterm victory and gives the incoming majority more room to block legislation and investigate the Trump administration.

**Sources:** [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/us/democrat-scott-colom-defeats-incumbent-cindy-hyde-smith-cnn-projects-2026-11-11/)

## 3. White House warns Democratic investigations could become criminal

White House counsel Will Scharf told incoming House Oversight Committee chair Robert Garcia that planned investigations of the Trump administration could cross into criminal conduct if they used threats, abusive processes or unconstitutional subpoenas. Garcia rejected the warning as intimidation and said the committee would continue its work when Democrats take control of Congress in January.

**Why it matters:** The exchange raises the prospect of a direct institutional confrontation between the executive branch and a newly empowered Congress before the next legislative term begins.

## 4. Taiwan demands equal participation at China's APEC summit

President Lai Ching-te said Taiwan would attend next week's APEC leaders' summit in Shenzhen on an equal basis and named Acer founder Stan Shih as its envoy. Beijing said Taiwan's participation must follow the one-China principle and APEC arrangements, while Taipei said it would seek immediate correction of any unequal treatment.

**Why it matters:** The summit creates a high-profile test of whether cross-strait political tensions can be contained inside one of the few major international forums in which both Taiwan and China participate.

## 5. Hormuz shipping recovers as attacks reach a wartime high

Gulf LNG exports rose to their highest monthly level since the Iran war began, with 31 tankers leaving through the Strait of Hormuz in October, but the same month also brought a wartime high of 37 attacked vessels. International Maritime Organization data recorded 130 incidents and at least 25 seafarer deaths, while an oil slick from a damaged tanker threatened a major UAE desalination plant.

**Why it matters:** Higher traffic is improving energy flows but exposing more crews and critical coastal infrastructure to attack, showing that market optimism has run ahead of maritime security.

**Topics:** [Strait of Hormuz](../../Topics/Strait-of-Hormuz.md), [Iran](../../Topics/Iran.md)

**Sources:** [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/hormuz-traffic-is-up-so-are-attacks-2026-11-11/)

## 6. Switzerland preserves travel rights for Ukrainians with protection status S

The Federal Council sent parliament legislation allowing Ukrainians with protection status S to continue travelling abroad and returning to Switzerland without individual permission. Asylum seekers and other temporarily admitted people would generally face tighter restrictions, while travel to Ukraine by S-status holders would remain limited to 15 days per half-year.

**Why it matters:** The proposal preserves a special right aligned with EU practice while implementing travel restrictions that parliament approved for other categories of protected migrants.

## 7. Switzerland falls to ninth in revamped digital and AI ranking

Switzerland dropped from third to ninth in IMD's 2026 World Digital and AI Ranking, which now measures readiness to use and govern artificial intelligence across 70 economies. The country remained strong in workforce development and institutional values but ranked 13th in governance and technology infrastructure and 16th in efficiency.

**Why it matters:** The result highlights a widening gap between Switzerland's research and talent base and its capacity to finance, deploy and govern AI at scale.

## 8. UK-backed Fractile raises $720 million for AI inference chips

British AI-chip startup Fractile raised $720 million at a $7 billion valuation from investors including Thrive Capital, CapitalG and British public investment vehicles. The company says its inference chips can lower energy use and costs and plans to launch its first commercial product next year.

**Why it matters:** The financing gives Britain's effort to build sovereign AI infrastructure a major domestic hardware contender in a market dominated by Nvidia and Asian manufacturers.

## 9. Technology platforms oppose Indonesia's proposed AI copyright rules

Meta and TikTok joined Google in opposing proposed revisions to Indonesia's copyright law that would require platforms to pay publishers for displaying news and establish rules for artificial intelligence. If enacted, the measure could make Indonesia the first Southeast Asian country to address AI directly in copyright legislation.

**Why it matters:** The proposal brings the global dispute over compensation for news and the use of human-created works in AI training into a large emerging digital market.

## 10. Researchers say AI agents helped steal Tajik government documents

CloudSek researchers said a Kazakhstan-linked group used the open-source Hermes agent framework to steal more than 2,000 national-security documents from Tajikistan's electronic document system. The exposed operation showed the agent conducting reconnaissance, writing custom download software and organising stolen files, while Kazakh and Tajik officials did not immediately comment.

**Why it matters:** The case suggests that autonomous AI agents are moving beyond opportunistic cybercrime into sophisticated, state-linked espionage operations.
