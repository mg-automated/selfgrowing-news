## Overview

The first direct Russia–Ukraine talks in nine months produced constructive language and discussion of prisoner exchanges and security guarantees, but no breakthrough on the war's central disputes. ## 1. Russia and Ukraine complete first day of renewed US-mediated talks

Russian and Ukrainian delegations held nearly four hours of talks in Abu Dhabi, their first US-mediated negotiations since February. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the meeting productive and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy described it as constructive, with discussions covering a possible large prisoner exchange, detained civilians and children, and long-term security guarantees; a second session was scheduled for Wednesday.

**Why it matters:** The meeting reopened a direct diplomatic channel and identified practical areas for agreement, but the sides remain far apart over territory, NATO membership and the sequence of a ceasefire and wider security negotiations.

## 2. EU members clash over putting Ukraine support into the next budget

German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said support for Ukraine should be incorporated into the European Union's long-term budget beginning in 2028 and that every member state should contribute. Slovakia's Robert Fico accused Merz of blackmail, while Hungary's Peter Magyar questioned linking the issues; Merz said Ukraine faces an estimated €70 billion funding gap in 2027 and expects the EU to provide €45 billion.

**Why it matters:** The dispute tests whether the EU can turn political support for Ukraine into predictable collective financing despite increasingly divergent national positions.

## 3. Israel's new parliament takes office without a governing majority

Israel's newly elected Knesset was sworn in while Gadi Eisenkot continued trying to form a government after the October election left both his alliance and Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc short of a majority. Eisenkot has until December 6, with a possible extension to December 20, and his allies immediately proposed an inquiry into the October 7 attacks, prime-ministerial term limits and repeal of Netanyahu-era judicial changes.

**Why it matters:** Parliament can begin reshaping policy even before a coalition is formed, but prolonged deadlock could return the mandate to Netanyahu or force another election.

## 4. Sweden gives Kristersson another week to form a government

Swedish parliamentary speaker Andreas Norlén gave Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson another week to seek a governing majority after his right-wing bloc lost its majority in September. The Sweden Democrats dropped their demand for cabinet posts, potentially making Centre Party support easier, while opposition leader Magdalena Andersson also asked for another chance to assemble a coalition.

**Why it matters:** The extension keeps both possible governing blocs alive and makes the Centre Party's support pivotal to ending Sweden's post-election deadlock.

## 5. Thune and Jordan chosen to lead Republicans in minority

Republicans chose John Thune as Senate minority leader and Jim Jordan as House minority leader after losing control of both chambers in the midterm elections. Thune said Republicans would use the Senate filibuster to defend conservative priorities, while Jordan's elevation puts a combative Trump ally at the front of the party's House opposition.

**Why it matters:** The leadership choices will shape how effectively Democrats can legislate during the final two years of Donald Trump's presidency, especially where Senate rules still require Republican votes.

## 6. United States again skips UN review of its human-rights record

The United States left its seat empty for a scheduled Universal Periodic Review at the UN Human Rights Council in Geneva, prompting the council to postpone the examination until January. Washington said participation would legitimise a body it considers biased, while rights organisations had submitted concerns covering migration, racial discrimination, abortion and the death penalty.

**Why it matters:** Refusing a process applied to all UN member states weakens international scrutiny of US policy and may make it harder for Washington to press other governments on human-rights accountability.

## 7. Switzerland plans stronger drone protection for energy infrastructure

The Swiss Federal Office of Energy is preparing a legal framework that would require operators of critical energy infrastructure to protect sites against sabotage, drones and electromagnetic threats. Operators could reinforce buildings, install sensors and nets, and maintain spare parts, while proposals to allow interception drones or jammers would require further legal changes because the state retains the monopoly on force.

**Why it matters:** Assigning clearer duties to energy suppliers addresses a recognised protection gap that the Swiss armed forces say they lack the capacity to cover at every site.

## 8. TSMC approves investments in Anthropic and OpenAI

TSMC's board approved investments of up to $500 million each in Anthropic and OpenAI, giving the world's largest contract chipmaker direct stakes in two major customers for AI infrastructure. The decision came as Anthropic priced its planned initial public offering at $70 to $78 per share and TSMC said AI-chip demand continued to exceed manufacturing capacity.

**Why it matters:** TSMC is positioning itself to profit both from supplying AI hardware and from ownership in frontier-model developers, further linking the semiconductor and AI-laboratory sectors.

## 9. Alibaba's T-Head files for a Hong Kong listing

**Why it matters:** A separate listing could channel more capital into China's domestic AI-chip ecosystem and give investors direct exposure to a strategically important semiconductor developer.

## 10. CoreWeave targets adjusted profitability in 2028

CoreWeave said it expects to become profitable on an adjusted basis in the first half of 2028 as it tries to reassure investors about its debt-funded expansion. The company reported $46.9 billion in debt, a $132.5 billion revenue backlog and demand roughly ten times available capacity, while raising its 2026 revenue forecast and projecting $37 billion to $39 billion in annual spending.

**Why it matters:** CoreWeave's results demonstrate both extraordinary demand for AI computing and the financial risk created when infrastructure growth depends on heavy borrowing and sustained customer commitments.
