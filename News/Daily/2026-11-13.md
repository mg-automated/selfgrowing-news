## 1. Russia accepts European participation in next Ukraine talks

US envoy Steve Witkoff said President Vladimir Putin agreed that European representatives could join the next round of Ukraine peace negotiations, a move welcomed by President Volodymyr Zelenskiy. Major disputes remain over territory, security guarantees and Western troops, while the Kremlin stopped short of confirming the participation arrangement.

**Why it matters:** European participation could make any eventual security settlement more durable, but the contradictory Russian signals show that the expanded format is not yet settled.

## 2. China stages large unannounced Pacific military exercise

Taiwanese officials said China massed more than 100 naval and coast-guard vessels in early November for unannounced drills around Taiwan and across the western Pacific, including simulated attacks and a submarine-launched intercontinental ballistic missile test. The exercise involved three theatre commands and exceeded the scale of China's previous formally named Taiwan-focused drill, while Beijing described carrier activity as routine.

**Why it matters:** The scale and lack of public announcement suggest Beijing is testing blockade and far-seas capabilities while applying pressure before the APEC summit without triggering the response associated with a declared exercise.

## 3. European Union prepares another large Russia sanctions package

France said EU governments would approve sanctions on Monday against 150 additional entities and 50 banks linked to Russia. The announcement came as Paris condemned Moscow's decision to bar two French journalists and two lawmakers, which French officials described as intimidation and retaliation for action against alleged Russian influence activity.

**Why it matters:** The package shows Europe continuing to increase economic pressure while diplomacy over Ukraine expands, preserving leverage ahead of further negotiations.

**Topics:** [European Union](../../Topics/European-Union.md), [Russia](../../Topics/Russia.md), [European Security](../../Topics/European-Security.md)

## 4. Houthis claim attacks on Saudi Arabia's strategic oil corridor

Yemen's Houthis said they attacked a pumping station on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, an oil facility in Yanbu and an Aramco building in Riyadh. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted two Houthi drones; the pipeline currently carries about four million barrels per day to the Red Sea, helping bypass disruption in the Strait of Hormuz.

**Why it matters:** Successful attacks on the corridor could weaken one of the world's most important safeguards against Gulf supply disruption and extend the conflict toward the Red Sea.

**Topics:** [Houthis](../../Topics/Houthis.md), [Yemen Conflict](../../Topics/Yemen-Conflict.md), [Red Sea Shipping](../../Topics/Red-Sea-Shipping.md)

**Sources:** [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/middle-east/yemens-houthis-say-they-attacked-pumping-station-saudi-arabias-east-west-oil-2026-11-12/)

## 5. Senegal's budget impasse threatens a $2.2 billion IMF programme

The IMF said Senegal must complete an agreed budget framework, correct the misreporting of hidden debt and secure financing assurances before its board can approve a $2.2 billion programme. A parliamentary committee has asked the government to withdraw the 2027 budget, deepening a political dispute between President Bassirou Diomaye Faye and former prime minister Ousmane Sonko's parliamentary majority.

**Why it matters:** The programme is central to restructuring Senegal's debt after the discovery of roughly $13 billion in previously undisclosed liabilities, making the political deadlock a direct financial risk.

**Topics:** [Senegal](../../Topics/Senegal.md)

**Sources:** [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/africa/imf-says-senegal-must-implement-budget-framework-win-approval-22-billion-program-2026-11-12/)

## 6. Swiss finance committee recommends rejecting the 2027 budget

The National Council's Finance Committee voted 15–9 to recommend rejection of the federal government's 2027 budget after failing to agree on more than 100 proposed changes. The committee said the government had been too cautious, leaving parliament to begin deliberations with sharply divided majority and minority positions.

**Why it matters:** A rejection recommendation from the responsible committee signals an unusually difficult budget debate and increases uncertainty around federal spending priorities.

## 7. New EU unemployment rules could cost Switzerland up to CHF800 million annually

The European Commission told Bern that responsibility for unemployment benefits for cross-border workers would shift from their country of residence to their last country of employment. SECO estimates the change could add CHF500–800 million a year to Swiss costs, and the National Council is urging the Federal Council to reject it.

**Why it matters:** The proposal creates a substantial new fiscal exposure tied to free movement and could become another contentious issue in Switzerland's wider relationship with the EU.

## 8. Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia chief executive

Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang resigned after reports of extramarital relationships, including one involving a businesswoman suspected by US authorities of links to Chinese intelligence, although authorities have not concluded she was a spy. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress became interim CEO as lawmakers pressed for tighter China chip controls and Nvidia shares closed 4.3% lower.

**Why it matters:** Huang's departure removes the central strategist from the company that anchors the AI-computing ecosystem and could reshape both Nvidia's investment policy and US export controls.

## 9. Lawsuit seeks to halt frontier AI development

Public Citizen and other nonprofit groups sued OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google and SpaceX's AI division in California, seeking an injunction against further development of their most advanced models. The complaint invokes state unfair-business-practice and public-nuisance law, citing company leaders' own warnings and reported incidents involving autonomous models.

**Why it matters:** The case tests whether existing state law can impose sweeping restrictions on frontier development in the absence of comprehensive federal AI regulation.

## 10. FBI says North Korean hackers stole more than $400 million

The FBI said North Korean state-backed hackers stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over the past 18 months using secretly recorded images of people to target their contacts. The disclosure adds a social-engineering method to the growing catalogue of state-backed cryptocurrency theft attributed to Pyongyang.

**Why it matters:** Cryptocurrency theft supplies hard currency to an isolated state while the use of captured imagery makes trusted personal contacts a new attack surface.
