## 1. European Union sanctions Russian banks as it accuses Moscow of delaying peace

EU foreign ministers imposed sanctions on nearly 50 Russian banks and financial institutions, alongside asset freezes and travel bans targeting energy-linked businesspeople and alleged propagandists. The ministers accused Russia of prolonging Ukraine peace negotiations while attacking power infrastructure ahead of winter and said a ceasefire should precede a broader settlement.

**Why it matters:** The measures increase economic pressure on Moscow while reinforcing Europe's demand for a negotiating process that does not reward continued attacks on Ukraine.

## 2. Ethiopia and Sudan trade accusations of supporting armed groups

Sudan's military accused Ethiopia of allowing Sudanese paramilitary forces to establish camps, operate drones and launch cross-border attacks from Ethiopian territory. Ethiopia in turn accused Sudan's military of supporting rebels fighting federal forces in Tigray, where renewed fighting has undermined the 2022 peace agreement.

**Why it matters:** Reciprocal support for armed groups could connect two major internal conflicts and turn instability along the shared border into a wider regional confrontation.

## 3. Pakistan and regional partners keep US–Iran mediation alive

Pakistan's foreign minister said Islamabad would continue efforts to reduce US–Iran tensions with support from other members of the Economic Cooperation Organization. The organisation's meeting endorsed mediation by Pakistan and Qatar and an Iranian initiative, while Pakistan said it was waiting for a US response to Tehran's latest proposals.

**Why it matters:** Continued regional mediation offers a diplomatic channel despite renewed attacks and the absence of a comprehensive settlement.

## 4. Swiss committee seeks mandatory cantonal vote on EU treaty package

The House of Representatives' constitutional-affairs committee recommended that the stabilisation section of Switzerland's proposed EU treaty package require approval by both voters and a majority of cantons. The committee adopted the motion by 14 votes to 11, aligning with the Senate's position ahead of a decision by the full house during the winter session.

**Why it matters:** Requiring a double majority would raise the political threshold for approving the treaty package and could materially affect Switzerland's future relationship with the EU.

## 5. Investigation finds Swiss ammunition reached Russia through intermediaries

An investigation based on Russian conformity records found that precision ammunition supplied by state-owned RUAG to Slovenia was subsequently sold through a private dealer and Kyrgyzstan before reaching Russia. Switzerland's State Secretariat for Economic Affairs said it was examining the case, which emerged shortly before a national vote on revising the War Materiel Act.

**Why it matters:** The case exposes enforcement gaps in Swiss arms-export controls and directly informs the debate over whether re-export safeguards should be relaxed.

## 6. More than 800 Swiss hotels join Booking.com damages claim

More than 800 Swiss hotels have joined a European claim involving 18,135 establishments that seeks compensation from Booking.com over long-running price-parity clauses. The claimants argue that the clauses restricted competition by preventing hotels from offering better prices elsewhere; Booking.com disputes the allegations, and an initial hearing is expected in 2027.

**Why it matters:** The case could produce substantial damages and reshape the contractual power of dominant digital booking platforms across Europe.

## 7. Zurich Airport begins three-year autonomous-wheelchair trial

Zurich Airport will operate four self-driving wheelchairs for three years on designated routes between an airside waiting area and gates in Dock A. Each chair uses more than 30 sensors to detect obstacles and brake automatically, while the airport says the service will supplement rather than replace personal assistance.

**Why it matters:** The trial tests how autonomous mobility technology can increase passenger independence and free staff for travellers needing more intensive support.

## 8. United States tightens controls on remote access to advanced AI chips

The Trump administration introduced new licensing requirements for advanced-chip shipments to data centres in Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Brazil, and for overseas facilities providing significant computing capacity to China-based companies. The rule targets remote access and diversion routes that Chinese firms have used to train AI models without importing restricted chips directly.

**Why it matters:** Closing overseas-compute loopholes broadens US export controls from physical chips to access to computing power and could accelerate technological separation between the United States and China.

## 9. ECB says AI has become a systemic financial-stability risk

European Central Bank Vice President Boris Vujcic said AI-related risks now extend beyond high equity valuations into debt markets, cybersecurity and operational safety. He highlighted concentrated exposures, opaque financing structures, autonomous cyberattacks and an estimated $5.5 trillion financing need among major US technology companies through 2030.

**Why it matters:** The assessment signals that central banks increasingly view the AI investment boom as a potential source of system-wide financial and operational stress.

## 10. Federal Reserve chair frames AI as both a productivity opportunity and employment risk

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh said widespread adoption of emerging technologies could lift productivity and long-term growth but might also increase unemployment or slow the economy if investment returns disappoint. He said the Fed must prepare for a wide range of outcomes because the scale and timing of AI's economic effects remain uncertain.

**Why it matters:** The remarks place AI adoption inside monetary-policy analysis, linking technology investment to growth, labour markets and future financial conditions.
